What is home title fraud?

DeedGuard USA · Reviewed by a Florida real estate attorney · Updated November 2026

Home title fraud — also called deed fraud or seller-impersonation fraud — is when a criminal forges a deed to transfer, sell, or borrow against a home they don't own. The forged document is recorded in public land records, and on paper the property now belongs to someone else. The real owner usually doesn't find out for months.

How home title fraud works

Property ownership is a public record. Anyone can look up your deed at the county, and — with a forged signature and a notary stamp — a fraudster can record a new document that appears legitimate:

  • They forge a deed (often a quitclaim deed) transferring your home to themselves or a shell company.
  • They record it at the county clerk's office, where recorders generally cannot verify that signatures are genuine.
  • They cash in — taking out a mortgage or HELOC against your equity, selling the home to an unsuspecting buyer, or evicting your tenants.
  • You find out late — through a foreclosure notice, a tax or loan statement, or a buyer showing up at your door.

Who criminals target

The best targets are homes nobody is actively watching:

  • Vacant land and second homes — no mail, no tenants, no one checking.
  • Snowbird and vacation properties — owners are away for months (a major issue in Florida).
  • Paid-off homes — lots of equity to borrow against, and no mortgage lender watching the title.
  • Rental and inherited property — ownership can be unclear and oversight is thin.
  • Elderly owners — frequently targeted by impersonation scams.

Why it's so hard to catch

Two things work against homeowners. First, the record is public and self-service — the system that lets you prove you own your home also lets a forger file a fake transfer. Second, the owner's title insurance most people buy at closing has, for over 20 years, excluded forgery that happens after the policy date (Exclusion 3(d)). So the coverage you assume protects you often doesn't apply to this exact crime — unless you add the newer ALTA 49/49.1 forgery endorsement, where it's available.

How to protect yourself

You can't stop someone from submitting a document to the county — but you can catch it fast and respond before the damage is done:

  1. Monitor the public record for any new filing on your property (deeds, mortgages, liens, judgments).
  2. Act within days if something appears — a quiet-title action and notice to the county can unwind a fraudulent filing.
  3. Consider the ALTA 49 / 49.1 endorsement where it's sold, to add real forgery coverage.
  4. Keep a real estate attorney on call so you're not scrambling to find one during a crisis.

Frequently asked questions

Is home title fraud common?
It's one of the fastest-growing real-estate crimes. The FBI has reported hundreds of millions of dollars in annual losses from real-estate and rental fraud, and title/forgery claims average well over $100,000 each.
Does title insurance cover it?
A standard owner's policy generally covers forgery from before your policy date but has historically excluded post-closing forgery (Exclusion 3(d)). The newer ALTA 49/49.1 endorsements can add that coverage where available.
How would I know if it happened?
Often you wouldn't — for months — until a foreclosure notice, a surprise loan, an eviction, or a buyer at the door. Monitoring public records for new filings is how you catch it early.
