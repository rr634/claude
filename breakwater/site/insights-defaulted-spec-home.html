A lender's playbook for a defaulted, half-finished waterfront spec home.

A half-built luxury home is the hardest kind of collateral to recover value from. Here is how to think about the options — and avoid the traps that quietly erode the balance every month.

Breakwater Capital Partners · South Florida

← All insights

When a construction loan on a luxury spec home goes into default at 60–80% complete, the lender inherits a problem that behaves nothing like a finished-home REO. The collateral isn't a house yet — it's a permit, a partial structure, a stack of liens, and a countdown. Carrying costs, deterioration, and lien priority erode the recovery every month it sits. The instinct to "foreclose and sell as-is" usually destroys the most value. Here's the fuller menu.

First, understand what you're actually holding

Before choosing a path, get clear on four things. These determine every option below:

  • Completion percentage and quality. Is the remaining work cosmetic finish, or structural and systems? A home at 80% with only finishes left is a very different asset from one at 55% with the roof and mechanicals unresolved.
  • The lien stack. Mechanic's liens, unpaid subs, a second mortgage, unpaid taxes. Their size and priority decide whether a clean title is weeks or months away.
  • Permit and inspection status. Are permits active or lapsed? A lapsed permit or a failed inspection can add months and six figures before a nail is driven.
  • The real finished value. Not the original pro forma — today's finished comp set in that exact submarket, haircut for time to sell.

The options, worst to best for recovery

1. Foreclose and sell as-is

The default reflex, and usually the weakest outcome. A half-finished luxury home at auction draws a thin, opportunistic buyer pool that prices in every unknown — lien risk, completion risk, carrying cost — and bids accordingly. You convert an uncertain recovery into a certain discount. It is fast, but it is where the most value leaks out.

2. Appoint a receiver to stabilize

A receiver can secure the site, stop deterioration, and bring order — valuable when the borrower is uncooperative or the asset is exposed. But a receiver stabilizes; it rarely completes. You've stopped the bleeding without creating the finished product that commands full value. It's often a bridge, not a destination.

3. Note sale to a completion buyer

Selling the defaulted note to a buyer who can actually finish the project moves the asset off your books quickly and at a price that reflects real completion economics rather than auction fear. The right counterparty is one who can take control, clear the liens, and carry the build to a certificate of occupancy — because that is the buyer who can pay the most and close with certainty.

4. Deed-in-lieu to a counterparty who completes and shares the upside

Where the borrower is cooperative, a negotiated deed-in-lieu to a completion operator — paired with a structure that gives the lender a secured or preferred position in the finished-home proceeds — can recover more than a note sale. You skip a contested foreclosure timeline and keep exposure to the completion spread instead of discounting it away.

The buyer who can finish the home is the buyer who can pay you the most — and close with the most certainty.

The traps that quietly cost you

  • Waiting. Every month of indecision adds carry, invites weather and vandalism damage, and lets liens and interest compound. Time is almost never on the lender's side with a stalled build.
  • Trusting the borrower's cost-to-complete. Distressed completion budgets are where recoveries die. The number must be independently scoped — the borrower's figure is usually optimistic by design.
  • Selling to someone who can't actually finish. A buyer who can acquire but not complete will re-trade you, stall, or walk — and you are back where you started, months later and deeper underwater.
  • Ignoring the lien stack until closing. Title problems surface at the worst possible moment. They should be mapped on day one, not discovered at the table.

The bottom line

The highest recovery on a defaulted, half-finished luxury home almost always comes from getting it into the hands of a counterparty who can take control cleanly, resolve the title and lien issues, finish the construction, and sell the finished product — and who can do all of that under one roof, fast, with certainty of close. The faster that counterparty is engaged, the less value erodes in the meantime.
